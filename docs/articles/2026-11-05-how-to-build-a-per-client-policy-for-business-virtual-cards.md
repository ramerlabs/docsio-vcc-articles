A strong per-client card policy gives each customer a clear spending boundary without forcing your team to share one card, chase receipts, or guess who approved a charge. The practical approach is to issue one business virtual card or card group per client, define exactly what it can pay for, set a budget and review date, and document who may use it.

This structure works especially well for agencies, freelancers, media buyers, e-commerce operators, and SaaS teams managing several accounts. It also makes payment failures easier to diagnose. When a subscription, ad account, or supplier charge fails, you can identify the affected client and card immediately instead of searching through a mixed transaction history. Use the policy below as a starting template, then adapt it to your provider’s controls, your contracts, and the client’s approval process.

## Why a per-client card policy reduces financial and operational risk

Client spending becomes difficult to control when multiple customers share one physical card or one unrestricted virtual card. A single card may contain advertising charges, software subscriptions, contractor expenses, and supplier payments. Even if the total balance is correct, the records are hard to allocate and a mistaken charge can affect every client connected to the account.

A per-client policy creates a separate control layer. The card is linked to one client, campaign, project, or approved expense category. Its name, limit, merchant restrictions, and owner should make that relationship obvious. If your team uses [business virtual cards](https://vccbusiness.com/), the goal is not simply to create more card numbers. The goal is to create an auditable system for authorization, funding, monitoring, and closeout.

There are also limits to what cards can solve. A card policy cannot replace a signed client agreement, accurate bookkeeping, or platform compliance. It should not be used to conceal spend, bypass a merchant’s verification process, or avoid a platform’s rules. Use the card as a controlled payment instrument, not as a substitute for governance.

## Use this per-client card policy template

Copy the following structure into your operations handbook, client onboarding checklist, or finance workspace. Fill in every field before issuing a card. If a field does not apply, write not applicable rather than leaving it blank.

> **Client and account:** Client legal or trading name, internal client ID, contract owner, billing contact, and relevant ad account, store, or workspace.**Card purpose:** State the permitted use in one sentence, such as approved social advertising for Client A’s summer campaign or software subscriptions listed in the monthly technology schedule.**Permitted merchants and categories:** List approved platforms, suppliers, merchant category types, domains, or payment processors. State whether the card may be used for one-time purchases, recurring charges, or both.**Budget and limits:** Record the spending limit, replenishment amount, time period, transaction cap, and whether unused funds may roll forward. Specify the currency and whether tax, fees, and foreign exchange costs count toward the limit.**Authorized users:** Name the employee, contractor, or team responsible for using the card. Explain whether credentials may be shared. The default should be no shared credentials unless your provider supports controlled access.**Approval rule:** Define which expenses require written client approval, internal approval, or both. Include the approval channel, such as a ticket, email thread, or project management record.**Evidence and reconciliation:** Require a receipt, invoice, campaign reference, or other business purpose for every charge. Set the deadline for attaching evidence and the person responsible for weekly or monthly reconciliation.**Review and expiry:** Record the issue date, next review date, contract end date, and the exact event that triggers suspension or closure.**Exception process:** Explain how the user requests a higher limit, a new merchant, or a temporary exception. No exception should take effect until an authorized reviewer records approval.

This template is intentionally specific. A phrase such as use for client expenses is too broad to control behavior. A phrase such as use only for approved Google Ads campaigns under account 12345, up to the documented weekly limit, is much easier to review.

## Choose the right card structure: client, campaign, or category

There is no universal answer to how many cards each client should receive. Choose the smallest structure that gives you reliable control. A single card per client is usually the simplest starting point for a freelancer or small agency. It keeps reconciliation manageable and avoids a large inventory of active numbers.

A separate card per campaign is better when a client has materially different budgets, approval owners, or platforms. For example, paid search and paid social may have different managers and spending ceilings. The drawback is administrative overhead: more cards must be funded, labeled, monitored, and closed.

A category-based card, such as one for software or suppliers, can work for internal expenses but is often less suitable for pass-through client spending. Category cards may combine several clients and weaken attribution. Use them only where your accounting process can reliably allocate each transaction.

**Decision rule:** Choose client-level cards when simplicity and attribution matter most. Choose campaign-level cards when budget isolation or approval separation matters more than convenience. Choose category-level cards only when the category is genuinely shared and the reconciliation owner has a dependable allocation process.

## Set limits that match the payment pattern

Limits should reflect how the card is used, not just the client’s total contract value. A recurring software card may need a stable ceiling that covers the expected subscription and reasonable tax or currency movement. An advertising card may need a replenishable balance, a daily transaction limit, and a review trigger when spend accelerates.

For predictable subscriptions, set the limit slightly above the approved recurring amount and review it after any pricing or seat change. For variable advertising, use a funding schedule tied to the approved media plan. Do not assume that a high account-level budget is the same as permission to spend: the card policy should state the approved period, campaign, and amount.

When a payment method must be funded repeatedly, review whether a [reloadable vcc](https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-vcc) fits the workflow. A reloadable instrument can be useful when the business wants to add funds as a campaign progresses rather than issue a new card for every billing cycle. Confirm the provider’s funding, balance, expiry, verification, and merchant acceptance rules before relying on it for a critical account.

For recurring tools, document the renewal date and owner. A guide to [virtual card recurring payments](https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-card-recurring-payments) can help your team think through subscription continuity, but the policy should still require monitoring for price increases, duplicate subscriptions, failed renewals, and services no longer used.

## Build the approval and evidence workflow

The policy works only when approval is easy enough to follow. For each client, assign one internal owner and one backup. The owner confirms that the payment is within scope, while the finance or operations reviewer checks the transaction evidence. On small teams, one person may perform both tasks, but the decision should still be recorded.

Use a consistent request format: client name, merchant, amount or expected range, purpose, campaign or project, date needed, and approver. A written approval should exist before the card is funded or the charge is made, except for narrowly defined emergency situations. Emergency spending should have a short deadline for retrospective review, not unlimited discretion.

Require evidence that matches the transaction. An invoice is appropriate for software; a platform receipt and campaign reference are more useful for advertising; a supplier order and delivery record may be needed for inventory. Screenshots alone may not prove what was purchased, so pair them with the transaction record and business purpose.

Keep client funds and company operating funds distinguishable in your records. Whether you bill in advance, reimburse expenses, or pay first and invoice later, your accounting treatment should be consistent and reviewed by the person responsible for your books.

## Use reloadable cards carefully for ongoing client spend

A reloadable arrangement can reduce card replacement work for campaigns and recurring supplier relationships, but it also creates a temptation to treat one card as a permanent pool of money. That weakens the policy if the card is reused for unrelated clients or projects.

If you use a [reloadable virtual credit card](https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-credit-card), label it with the client, purpose, and review date. Record every load and unload, the source of funds, the approved amount, and the person who authorized it. Set a maximum balance and suspend further funding when required evidence is missing.

A [reloadable virtual card](https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-card) may be practical for controlled online purchases, but check whether the merchant accepts the card type and whether recurring billing requires a stable authorization. Some merchants may perform small verification charges, delayed captures, deposits, or incremental authorizations. Your reconciliation process should account for these events rather than treating them automatically as errors.

For businesses that specifically require Visa acceptance, compare the operational requirements of a [virtual visa reloadable](https://vccbusiness.com/virtual-visa-reloadable) option with your merchants and provider controls. Do not choose based on the label alone. Check country availability, identity verification, loading methods, transaction limits, settlement timing, and support for the billing patterns your client actually uses.

## Actionable checklist before issuing any client card

Complete this checklist for every new card and repeat the review when the client, campaign, or payment pattern changes.

1. Confirm the client, contract, account, and billing owner are documented.
2. Write the card’s permitted purpose in one specific sentence.
3. Set the currency, spending limit, transaction cap, and active dates.
4. List approved merchants, platforms, domains, or expense categories.
5. Assign a primary user, backup user, approver, and reconciliation owner.
6. Define the evidence required for each major transaction type.
7. Record the suspension triggers, review date, and closure procedure.
8. Run a small approved test payment before committing a critical subscription or campaign.

## Common mistakes that undermine per-client controls

- **Creating cards without naming conventions:** A card labeled only with a provider-generated number is difficult to identify during an urgent review. Use a consistent format such as client, purpose, and month or campaign.
- **Setting limits too high for convenience:** An oversized limit may reduce declines but increases exposure if credentials are compromised or a merchant bills incorrectly.
- **Ignoring renewal behavior:** A card that was approved for one month may continue paying for many months unless expiry, subscription review, or a cancellation owner is documented.
- **Mixing clients on a reloadable balance:** Shared balances make refunds, disputes, and client reconciliation harder. Keep funding attributable wherever possible.
- **Allowing verbal exceptions:** A message such as just pay it this once becomes difficult to audit. Record the exception, amount, reason, approver, and expiry.
- **Assuming every merchant accepts every virtual card:** Test important merchants and understand whether they require recurring authorization, a physical card, address matching, or additional verification.
- **Closing cards before refunds settle:** Some refunds or reversals can take time. Confirm outstanding authorizations, refunds, and disputes before closure.

## When a per-client card is not the best choice

Do not create a separate card when the administrative burden outweighs the control benefit. A very small one-off purchase may be simpler through an approved company card with a clear project code and receipt. Likewise, if a merchant requires a specific payment method that your virtual card cannot support, forcing the transaction into the policy may create an avoidable failure.

A card is also a poor substitute for a formal procurement process when the purchase involves sensitive data, regulated services, employment obligations, or a significant supplier commitment. Those expenses may require legal, security, or finance review before payment. For high-value or long-term commitments, use a purchase order or contract approval workflow alongside the card controls.

Finally, do not use multiple cards to evade a merchant’s fraud controls, advertising restrictions, account limits, or identity checks. If a platform declines the payment or asks for verification, resolve the issue through the platform’s permitted process.

## FAQ about per-client business virtual cards

### Should each client have one card or several?

Start with one card per client when the client has a straightforward spending pattern. Add campaign-level or category-level cards only when separate budgets, users, or approval paths provide a real control benefit. Too many cards increase the chance of forgotten subscriptions, incorrect funding, and incomplete reconciliations. Review the structure after the first billing cycle and split it only when the existing card makes attribution or budget control unreliable.

### Can a client use the card directly?

Usually, the safer default is for your authorized team to control the card and pay approved expenses on the client’s behalf. Direct client access may be appropriate in a managed service arrangement, but document the user, permitted merchants, limit, and evidence requirements. Never share credentials informally. Use provider-supported access controls where available, and clarify in the client agreement who is responsible for unauthorized charges or missed receipts.

### How should recurring subscriptions be handled?

Record the merchant, subscription tier, renewal date, expected amount, owner, and cancellation rule. Review the subscription before renewal when pricing, seats, or scope can change. If the amount varies, set a reasonable ceiling and require approval for charges above it. Keep the card active until refunds, credits, and final invoices have settled, then close or suspend it promptly when the service ends.

### Are reloadable cards suitable for advertising budgets?

They can be suitable when the provider supports the merchant, the funding process is dependable, and the team monitors balance and authorization behavior. Use a separate card or balance for each client or campaign where practical. Define the approved spend period and replenishment authority. Test the payment method before launching an important campaign, and keep an alternative compliant payment method available for platform interruptions.

### What should happen when a client leaves?

Stop new funding immediately, pause or suspend the card, and review pending authorizations, refunds, disputes, and recurring charges. Export the transaction history and attach missing receipts before closing the client file. Cancel or transfer subscriptions according to the contract, then document the closure date and final balance. Retain records according to your accounting and contractual requirements rather than deleting them as soon as the card is closed.

On day one, list every active client-funded card and assign each one a clear owner. On days two and three, apply the policy template, rename cards, and record limits, merchants, renewal dates, and approval channels. On day four, review recurring charges and remove cards from services that no longer have a business purpose.

On day five, test your evidence workflow with a recent transaction and identify missing receipts or unclear approvals. On day six, review whether any client needs a [reloadable virtual visa card](https://vccbusiness.com/reloadable-virtual-visa-card) or another card structure for ongoing spend, while checking the provider’s terms and merchant acceptance. On day seven, publish the policy, train users on exceptions and suspension triggers, and schedule a monthly review.

The objective is a card system that makes the correct action obvious: the right client, the right merchant, the right limit, and the right evidence. That clarity is more valuable than simply issuing a larger number of cards.
